Meta and Microsoft now give each person an AI agent with its own machine in the cloud. Five questions worth asking that machine before you connect your accounts to it.

Until now, an AI agent was a program on your computer. Claude Code, Cursor or Codex run where you work, with your files and your keys, and what they are allowed to do is written in files that can be opened and read.

This autumn something else appeared. On 8 September Meta introduced Muse: each person gets a virtual machine of their own, with a browser, files and memory, where their agent lives. On 25 September Microsoft introduced Autopilot inside Copilot: an agent that lives in the company's tenant, with its own identity and computer, and keeps working when you are away.

The idea is the same in both: you say what you want, and the agent does it on a machine that is not yours, with your accounts connected.

What changes

On your computer, someone can look. That is what RosmarOps does: it reads your agents' configuration and tells you what each one can do without asking you.

Nobody from outside gets into the vendor's computer. The only thing you have to know what the agent will do is what the vendor has written in its documentation. If it is not there, you cannot know.

Who approves

There is one detail both share that deserves attention. When the agent is about to do something important, the one who decides whether it goes ahead is often not you: it is another AI model.

Meta calls it Sentinel and describes it as the only permission authority for actions with connected services and for everything that goes out to the network. Microsoft calls it the automated review system: a second AI model that reviews actions before they run.

It is not a design flaw; it is how the agent can work without stopping at every step. But it changes the usual question. “Does it ask me first?” is no longer enough. You need to know who answers when it does not ask you.

The five questions

They are the ones from the RosmarOps Control Baseline that can be asked of an agent running in the vendor's cloud. They are answered yes or no, by reading its documentation:

  1. Out of the box. Does it say what the agent can do without asking you, as it comes?
  2. The keys. Are they kept per connection, can they be removed one by one, and does it say whether the agent sees them?
  3. With nobody watching. Can you see the list of what it has scheduled and turn each item off?
  4. Stopping. Can you stop it, and stop everything at once?
  5. Security advisories. Does it publish its advisories in a fixed place and say how to report a flaw?

There are two more questions the baseline does not ask yet and that these products put on the table: who decides in your place, and whether you can read a record of what the agent sent out.

What you can do today

Before you connect your email, your calendar or your bank to an agent like this, look for those five answers in its help pages. If one is missing, you already know something: not that the answer is bad, but that you do not have it.
